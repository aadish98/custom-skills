# Putting together your presentation

Full text of the Allada lab talk guide (adapted from Indira M. Raman), preserved for reference.

## How to structure your talk

### a) The introduction

Try to bring your audience from the broad, general, easy-to-grasp concepts to the level of analysis at which you will present.

- Why is your work important?
- What were the original questions that provided the impetus for the work?
- What was the status of knowledge in the field before the work in question was performed?

Please avoid presenting the primary data (e.g., figures) from other groups/other labs and, for lab meeting, avoid presenting your own primary data from prior lab meetings, i.e., focus on new data — in these cases, it is most appropriate to summarize these data.

FOCUS your introduction on the background the audience NEEDS to know to understand your talk (no more, no less). Don't "just" provide background.

### b) Data presentation

If you follow the format below, data slides will almost always be broken up by other slides that discuss the rationale, results, and significance for particular experiments. By doing this, you will avoid simply regurgitating all of the experimental data without providing context for the experiments.

**1) Rationale for the experiment, i.e., why am I doing this?**

- Why did the preparation/technique you use serve as a good context for investigating the question?
- What advantages did it have over previous approaches?
- What question are you answering? Why is it important?
- Provide sufficient background to allow the audience to understand the experiments.

**2) Data**

- Explain the methodology used and the results obtained (this is generally not a problem).
- Present data from left to right, controls first (should I believe this experiment?).
- Use the appropriate statistics, if you claim something is significant/important.
- Label figures completely, clearly including labeling x and y axes.
- Avoid using Bloomington/other stock numbers and instead label genotypes (where genotypes are complicated indicate if "wild-type", "mutant", and "rescue").
- Avoid presentation of large tables or color code them to ease viewing.
- Pay attention to significant digits (for period should be xx.x, for P-S xxx., etc.). Try to remove extraneous digits.
- Minimize the number of panels on a single slide.

**3) Conclusions**

- Discuss the main conclusions of the experiment and their significance.
- How did the results that were obtained fit into or change the knowledge base?
- Which questions remain unanswered by the work, or which new questions arise? How might these be addressed with feasible (already available) experimental methods?

## How to present your talk

### Slides

- **Put affirmative conclusions at the top of each slide, i.e., what is the take-home message?** Titles should not be "The analysis of walking in flies" but rather "Flies exhibit two speeds of walking".
- Do not use titles such as "Methods" or "Results" — state the specific method or result instead.
- Slide titles should be minimum font size of 28.
- Do not talk about data/conclusions if they are not on your slides. The corollary is if it is important enough to discuss, put it on a slide.
- Do not put more than three or four main points on a slide. If you need to make more points, break it up into two or more slides. This prevents sticking on one slide for long periods of time.
- Bullet points or text should be brief.
- Use pictures rather than lists of statements wherever possible to illustrate your points. Pictures of data will often provide more information and nuance to your audience than your brief summary of the results.
- Whenever possible show your primary data. Do not only summarize what you think the results are.
- Data should be presented in the order (Left to Right) in which they are discussed with the control discussed first.
- X- and Y-axis labels as well as any figures/images should be as large as practically possible — avoid large amounts of white space.
- Either incorporate non-data figures (e.g. schematics or sentence slides) to summarize key points, or state the key points; in other words, have some sort of break between data slides to make sure your audience understands the main point (see above).

### Language

- Attend to the language used within the field: define any new, useful terms, and then use them.
- Make specific citations while speaking: Who did the work? When? How? What was the major result?
