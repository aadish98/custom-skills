# Allada lab presentation guide

Source: "Putting together a presentation," Allada lab talk guide (adapted from Indira M. Raman). Apply this skill when planning, drafting, or reviewing a scientific talk or lab meeting.

A good talk carries the audience from broad, easy concepts down to your specific analysis, and every slide states a conclusion rather than a topic.

## How to structure the talk

### Introduction

Bring the audience from broad, general, easy-to-grasp concepts down to the level of analysis you will present. Answer:

- Why is your work important?
- What original questions provided the impetus for the work?
- What was the status of knowledge in the field before this work?

Focus the introduction on the background the audience **needs** to understand your talk — no more, no less. Don't "just" provide background.

In lab meetings and talks, avoid presenting primary data (e.g., figures) from other groups, and avoid re-presenting your own primary data from prior lab meetings. Focus on new data; summarize the rest.

### Data presentation

Break data slides up with slides on rationale, results, and significance, so you give context instead of regurgitating every experiment. For each experiment, move through three parts:

1. **Rationale** — why am I doing this?
   - Why is the preparation or technique a good context for the question? What advantages does it have over previous approaches?
   - What question are you answering, and why does it matter?
   - Give enough background for the audience to understand the experiment.
2. **Data**
   - Explain the methodology and results.
   - Present data left to right, controls first ("should I believe this experiment?").
   - Use appropriate statistics for any claim of significance.
   - Label figures completely — including x and y axes.
   - Label genotypes rather than stock numbers; mark "wild-type," "mutant," or "rescue" when complicated.
   - Avoid large tables, or color-code them; keep significant digits meaningful; minimize panels per slide.
3. **Conclusions**
   - State the main conclusions and their significance.
   - How did the results fit into or change the knowledge base?
   - Which questions remain, or newly arise, and how could feasible experiments address them?

## How to present the talk

### Slides

- **Put the affirmative conclusion in the title** — the take-home message. Use "Flies exhibit two speeds of walking," not "The analysis of walking in flies."
- Don't use titles like "Methods" or "Results"; state the specific method or result.
- Slide titles: minimum 28-point font.
- Don't discuss data or conclusions that aren't on a slide; if it's worth discussing, put it on a slide.
- No more than three or four main points per slide; split further if needed.
- Keep bullets and text brief.
- Prefer pictures over lists; show primary data rather than only summarizing it.
- Order data left to right in the order you discuss them, control first.
- Make axis labels and images as large as practical; avoid large white space.
- Break up data slides with schematics or sentence slides that state the key point.

### Language

- Attend to the language of the field: define new, useful terms, then use them.
- Make specific citations while speaking: who did the work, when, how, and what the major result was.

## Review checklist

```
- [ ] Introduction moves broad → specific, and covers only needed background
- [ ] Each experiment has rationale, data, and conclusion slides
- [ ] Every slide title states an affirmative take-home message
- [ ] No "Methods"/"Results" titles; titles ≥ 28 pt
- [ ] Data shown left to right, controls first, axes fully labeled
- [ ] Genotypes labeled (not stock numbers); statistics appropriate
- [ ] ≤ 3–4 main points per slide; text brief; pictures over lists
- [ ] Primary data shown, not just summaries
- [ ] Break slides (schematic/sentence) separate data slides
- [ ] Field terms defined once, then used; citations spoken specifically
```
